# Cybersecurity Governance Framework

**Status:** Review draft 0.1  
**Publisher:** Joseph Technologies  
**Last reviewed:** 2026-07-18  
**Classification:** Original, unofficial implementation companion

This framework establishes a reusable governance model for directing, overseeing, and improving an organization's cybersecurity risk-management program. It must be tailored to the organization's mission, risk appetite, legal obligations, size, sector, and operating model.

It is not an official NIST, CISA, HHS, ISO, PCI SSC, FTC, or SEC publication. Adoption does not establish compliance or certification.

## 1. Purpose

The organization uses this framework to:

- connect cybersecurity decisions to enterprise objectives and risk;
- assign authority, accountability, and oversight;
- establish policy and control governance;
- maintain visibility into cybersecurity risk and performance;
- coordinate incident, resilience, privacy, supplier, and compliance activities;
- document risk decisions and drive continual improvement.

## 2. Scope

The framework applies to organizational units, personnel, information, technology, facilities, products, services, suppliers, and business processes included within the approved cybersecurity program scope.

The organization must document exclusions, the authority approving them, their rationale, related risk, and review date.

## 3. Governance principles

1. Cybersecurity risk is managed as part of enterprise risk—not solely as a technical issue.
2. Senior leadership establishes direction, risk expectations, resources, and accountability.
3. Decision rights and escalation paths are documented before high-impact events occur.
4. Security and privacy requirements are identified from applicable obligations and organizational risk.
5. Controls are selected and tailored; a catalog or crosswalk is not automatic proof of adequacy.
6. Risk decisions are evidence-based, authorized, time-bound, and reviewable.
7. Suppliers and other external dependencies are included in governance.
8. Incidents, assessments, monitoring, and changes feed continual improvement.

## 4. Governance structure

### Governing body or board

- oversees material cybersecurity risk within its assigned governance mandate;
- receives decision-useful reporting on risk, incidents, resilience, and program health;
- challenges management on material exposure, priorities, resources, and accepted risk;
- records governance decisions and requested follow-up.

### Executive management

- approves cybersecurity strategy, risk appetite, policies, priorities, and resources;
- assigns an accountable cybersecurity executive;
- resolves risk decisions beyond delegated authority;
- integrates cybersecurity with enterprise risk, legal, privacy, resilience, and business planning.

### Cybersecurity executive

- maintains the cybersecurity program and governance framework;
- advises decision-makers on threats, vulnerabilities, impacts, and treatment options;
- coordinates policy, architecture, control, incident, supplier, and assurance functions;
- escalates material risks and reports program performance.

### Risk owners

- own risk affecting their objectives, processes, systems, or data;
- approve treatment within delegated authority;
- provide resources and track remediation;
- seek higher authorization when residual risk exceeds authority.

### Control owners and system owners

- define and document implementation responsibility;
- maintain evidence about control design and operation;
- address findings and material changes;
- support assessment and monitoring.

### Independent assurance

- evaluates governance, risk management, and control effectiveness independently of implementation;
- reports findings through an appropriate assurance channel;
- does not assume management's risk ownership.

## 5. Governance lifecycle

### Establish context

- identify mission, stakeholders, assets, dependencies, obligations, and risk assumptions;
- define governance scope, authority, and risk criteria;
- maintain applicable legal, regulatory, contractual, and policy requirements.

### Assess risk

- identify credible threat scenarios and affected objectives;
- analyze likelihood, impact, existing safeguards, uncertainty, and dependencies;
- assign accountable risk owners;
- record inherent and residual risk using an approved methodology.

### Decide and treat

- select avoidance, mitigation, transfer/sharing, or acceptance actions;
- assign treatment owners, resources, milestones, and evidence requirements;
- authorize residual risk at the correct level;
- make exceptions explicit, compensating, time-bound, and reviewable.

### Implement and assure

- select and tailor controls based on risk and applicable requirements;
- document how controls are implemented and inherited;
- assess design and operation with appropriate independence;
- track deficiencies through corrective-action or POA&M processes.

### Monitor and improve

- monitor threats, changes, control status, incidents, suppliers, and risk indicators;
- report information suited to each decision-making level;
- review strategy, policies, risks, and exceptions on defined schedules and after material events;
- convert lessons learned into accountable improvements.

## 6. Policy and standards governance

The organization should maintain a controlled hierarchy:

1. governance framework and charter;
2. enterprise policies;
3. mandatory standards and baselines;
4. procedures and playbooks;
5. records, evidence, and operating artifacts.

Each controlled document should identify owner, approver, scope, effective date, review date, version, exceptions, related documents, and change history.

## 7. Risk decision governance

Risk acceptance must include:

- a defined risk statement and affected objectives;
- current controls and material gaps;
- assessed likelihood, impact, and uncertainty;
- treatment alternatives considered;
- residual risk and acceptance rationale;
- accountable owner and approving authority;
- expiration or next-review date;
- monitoring triggers and escalation conditions.

## 8. Incident governance

Before an incident, the organization should define:

- declaration and severity authority;
- incident command and succession;
- legal, privacy, regulatory, insurer, law-enforcement, supplier, and communications coordination;
- materiality and notification decision processes;
- evidence and decision-record requirements;
- executive and governing-body escalation thresholds;
- post-incident review and corrective-action ownership.

## 9. Supplier governance

Supplier governance should address risk classification, due diligence, contract requirements, access and data handling, concentration risk, continuous monitoring, incident coordination, reassessment, exceptions, and offboarding.

## 10. Reporting

Reports should be tied to decisions rather than activity volume. Each metric should state its owner, definition, calculation, source, frequency, target or threshold, limitations, and intended decision.

Suggested reporting domains:

- material risks and accepted risk;
- strategic objectives and roadmap;
- control effectiveness and significant deficiencies;
- incidents and operational resilience;
- supplier exposure;
- policy exceptions and overdue actions;
- workforce and resource constraints;
- regulatory and contractual exposure.

## 11. Minimum governance records

- approved charter and framework;
- roles and decision-authority matrix;
- strategy and roadmap;
- risk methodology, appetite, register, treatments, and acceptances;
- policy inventory and approvals;
- control inventory, implementation records, assessments, and POA&Ms;
- supplier-risk decisions;
- incident decisions and after-action records;
- metrics definitions and governance reports;
- exceptions, approvals, expirations, and reviews.

## 12. Review triggers

Review the framework at least on the organization's approved schedule and after material changes to strategy, regulation, technology, threat conditions, organizational structure, suppliers, or incident experience.
